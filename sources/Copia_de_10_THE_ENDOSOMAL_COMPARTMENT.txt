** 1. TYPES OF ENDOCYTOSIS 
The endosomal compartments are usually generated by endocytic vesicles.  Endosomes: any vesicle inside the cell, most of them arrive from endocytosis. Early endosomes are (many) originated by fusion of endocytic vesicles. These early endosomes may undergo maturation and become late endosomes.
They fuse with each other and segregate products. Eukaryotic cells are continuously taking up fluid and small molecules, by endocytosis. New endosomes are constantly emerging. They are in constant communication with each other, with the plasma membrane and with organelles such as the Golgi apparatus. Therefore, we can say that the endosomal compartment is very dynamic. 
The routes inward from the cell surface start with this process, by which cells take up plasma membrane components, fluid, solutes, macromolecules and particulate substances. Through endocytosis, the cell regulates the composition of its plasma membrane in response to the change of extracellular conditions. 
There are five different types of endocytosis or ways of internalizing contents from the extracellular medium by vesicles: most converge at the early endosome level (not phagocytosis)
Phagocytosis: Specific ingestion and degradation of very large particles. This is the only exception within the endocytosis pathways, since they don’t go through the usual early endosome sorting process.
Macropinocytosis: Non-specific uptake of large extracellular volumes
Receptor/Clathrin-mediated endocytosis: Specific uptake of ligands by binding to specific receptors.
Caveolin-mediated endocytosis: Non-specific 
Pinocytosis: Non-specific (naked vesicles)
**Non-specific: caveolin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis and pinocytosis. → it takes whatever is around
**Specific: phagocytosis and Clathrin-mediated endocytosis. 
Phagocytosis and macropinocytosis require the polymerization of the actin cytoskeleton to generate the pseudopodes that will surround those particles. 
Then, we have other “classic” endocytosis; Clathrin-mediated endocytosis, Caveolin-mediated endocytosis and Pinocytosis: the dynamine strangles the neck of the vesicle allowing the separation from the membrane (they form small vesicles). 
**Regardless of the pathway of those phagosomes, they will fuse together to form a dynamic early endosome. 
**1.1 Phagocytosis 
It’s the ingestion and degradation of very large particles (big bacteria, non biological material: large particles of dust) and it can only be performed by certain cells: phagocytic cells of the immune system (macrophage, dendritic cells, neutrophils). 
**This involves the polymerization of actin filaments at the edge of the membrane to create pseudopods. 
The particles that are phagocytized are so large that pseudopodia will surround the particle. Once the pseudopodial edges fuse with each other, the phagosome is formed (a very large endocytic vesicle). 
Once the phagosome is formed, it goes straight to degradation. It fuses with the lysosomes and the lysosome's hydrolytic enzymes get into the phagosome, resulting in the phagolysosome, which is responsible for breaking down the ingested content.
Phagocytosis relies on the specific identification (recognition). Particles (like bacteria) are often specifically recognized through receptors. For example, bacteria usually have antibodies or complement proteins attached, and phagocytes have receptors for antibodies or complement that allow them to recognize and internalize them. There are also specific bacterial receptors, like Toll-like receptors (TOL-like receptors), which recognize molecular patterns to internalize these materials. Identification is also called opsonization. 
Opsonization can be carried out by antibodies and specific receptors. The large particles that are internalized can be microorganisms (bacteria) which are very often marked by antibodies. In that case, phagocytic cells have antibiotic receptors to recognize those antibodies. Foreign materials (such as foreign beings) can also be recognized and sent to phagosomes.

Phagocytic cells are very rich in lysosomes, because this is actually one of their main functions, to degrade the contents internalized by phagocytosis. 
In this image we can see a neutrophil (a very common blood cell), with the nucleus in the bottom left side and the main characteristic here is the presence of  phagosomes which are phagocytizing bacteria (B). In late states of degradation the bacteria separates from its wall. Basically, those phagosomes will fuse with lysosomes (Ly) and hydrolytic enzymes will be introduced to this phagolysosome where degradation of the bacteria will occur. Pp is pseudopodia, an extended form of the cell to internalize bacteria. 
**1.2 Macropinocytosis 
Macropinocytosis is completely non-specific: a pseudo-volume is formed and whatever is around it enters. It involves the reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton (polymerization of actin filaments). It first generates pseudopods or membrane extensions, which fuse with the plasma membrane to capture large volumes of structure and extracellular fluid. This creates a very large endosome calle macropinosome (can have 1 micrometer in diameter). This macropinosomes will merge with other vesicles.
**1.3 Receptor mediated endocytosis (Clathrin-mediated endocytosis)
It’s very common in eukaryotic cells as it is a very effective way to internalize solutes specifically. This type of endocytosis involves the formation of clathrin coated vesicles (CCV) and the local polymerization of clathrin (a cytosolic protein) in order to facilitate the uptake of specific ligands upon binding to the receptors. It's a very important mechanism for capturing specific nutrients or other ligands in a controlled and specific way
The receptors and their ligands are simultaneously concentrated in specific patches of the plasma membrane, where CVVs are formed and invaginated to form endocytic vesicles. To do this, they require the help of other proteins. Once the endocytic vesicles form, the clathrin coats come apart. 
Wherever there is a large concentration of receptors Clathrin will go there. Ligands are internalized by binding to the receptors. That induces the polymerization of clathrin monomers underneath that membrane side (the clathrin monomers are called triskelions). 
The polymerization of clathrin endoplasmic facilitates endocytosis. Both the receptors and ligands will be internalized simultaneously creating a coated vesicle. This process is selective. 
In these images we can see receptors and ligands: only the ligands that are specific will go to the  receptors to be internalized. In the left image, the electron dense is the clathrin coat, so clathrin is  polymerizing at this level. 
Once the vesicle gets formed, dynamine participates to strangle the neck of the vesicle to separate from the plasma membrane. At the very early stage we have the depolymerization of clathrin, so the monomers go back to the cytosol to be ready to be reused. 
Many of these formed vesicles fuse with each other to generate early endosomes As early endosomes form, their lumen gradually becomes more acidic (the pH drops from about 7 in the environment to 6).  Because of the pH drop, the receptors and ligands dissociate and separate from each other inside the early endosome. There are proton pumps in some of these endosomes that cause the acidification of the endosomal lumen (the proton pumps are higher as the endosome matures) 
This separation of ligands and receptors happens at the early endosomal level: 
- Ligands will be stored in the early endosomes, then these will fuse with each other to generate late endosomes. Those late endosomes will move to the center of the cell and the final fate will be to fuse with lysosomes to undertake the degradation of the products inside. The inside will become more acidic with time from ph=6 to 5 and then 4.5
- Receptors are sent back to the plasma membrane to be reused, which is key to preventing the degradation of useful proteins that the cell can use again.
*Although it is not seen in the image, clathrin will also participate in intra-endosomal trafficking: the generation of the recycling endosome from the previous early endosome. However, once the vesicle gets formed, it depolymerizes. Exocytosis is also mediated by clathrins
**1.4. Caveolin- dependent endocytosis (non-specific) 
It is a non-specific endocytosis that takes portions of the cellular fluid or surrounding components. Caveolae are specific types of endothelial vesicles that contain caveolins, which are transmembrane proteins capable of oligomerizing and curving the membrane. When a plasma membrane domain has a high concentration of caveolins, they tend to oligomerize and spontaneously form endocytic vesicles, without the need for complex protein coats underneath or the involvement of specific receptors.
They play a critical role in cells specialized in nutrient absorption, such as:
Intestinal cells (from the intestinal epithelium).
Cells of blood capillaries (endothelial/ciliated cells), which have a large number of caveolae to sample nutrients present in the lumen of blood vessels.
This type of endocytosis is mediated by caveolin vesicles, which are non-coated (uncoated or naked) vesicles, this means, they don’t have any coat underneath the membrane. The vesicles are formed by the oligomerization of caveolin proteins (they bind to each other). The membrane gets bent and here we also have the participation of dynamin to separate the vesicle from the plasma membrane. This is one common thing between clathrin protein vesicles and caveolae (they both require dynamin). 
In the left image, we can see the Clathrin-Coated Vesicles (marked by arrow-heads with holes in the outside part) and caveolae vesicles (the naked ones). They can be distinguished by freeze etching because clathrin-coated vesicles have a coat, whereas caveolae vesicles are naked vesicles. On the right side, we have the TEM image of an endothelial cell. They are very thin and they form the capillary lumen. We can see an intense formation of caveolae.
**TRANSCYTOSIS 
Those caveoles are particularly abundant in cells that perform transcytosis (epithelial cells like endothelial cells of blood vessels, enterocytes, and intestinal epithelial cells).  Caveolae are abundant in endothelial cells, which are the ones that make the wall of the capillary, because they participate in the movement of nutrients from the capillary lumen to the peripheral tissues. In order to do that, caveolae are created on one side of the membrane, and then they fuse with the opposite side of it. The process is known as transcytosis, and it is a specific type of endocytosis whereby once the vesicle gets formed, it immediately gets released on another membrane domain. The vesicle will take the name of transcytotic vesicle. 
As we have already mentioned, this is a very typical process in endothelial cells. It is also common in enterocytes, where many of the nutrients from the lumen are transferred directly from the gut to peripheral tissues by means of transcytotic vesicles. 
**2. SORTING OF PRODUCTS AT THE EARLY ENDOSOME
**2.1. Destination of the products 
The sorting of products occurs by early endosomes. The early endosome is very dynamic. As we have seen, many of the products that it internalizes have to be sorted out: the receptors have to be sent to the plasma membrane, ligands to lysosomes… So once internalized, endosomal products have different possible destinations and that destination will be decided at the early endosome level: 
1. Recycling: The recycling vesicles go from one member to another. Once the products get internalized, some of them may be sent back to the PM, which is very useful in the case of receptors, since in that way there is no need to create more. 
*Experimental observation of recycling (TEM): Immunogold (Separation and sorting of receptors and ligands in early endosomes)  What we see is the separation of receptors and ligands. One way to visualize this process is to label each of them with gold particles of different sizes; antibodies can be used to bring those gold particles (gold dots), so that you can label receptors with the small particles and ligands with the large ones. Those particles will be visualized by TEM.
In the image, we can see each gold particle as a dot. At the early endosome we can see big dots together with small dots. Small dots are ligands and the big ones are receptors. As the endosome matures, the large particles go to one side of the endosome, whereas the rest of them remain inside. That shows the segregation of receptors to one part of the endosome, and it will culminate in the formation of the recycling endosomes (we can see big dots going away from the endosome because they are undergoing recycling). In that way, it is possible to avoid constantly creating new ones. At the end we will have only ligands stored in the early endosome. 
An example of receptor recycling: Cholesterol uptake. 
In the next image, we have the case of colesterol. Cholesterol is hydrophobic so it must travel through blood linked to lipoproteins. These have an hydrophobic pore containing cholesterol and some polar proteins facing the extracellular medium to make it soluble. Cholesterol travels bound to lipoproteins, and this complex is internalized as a hole through specific receptors and then by traffic coated vesicles. At the early endosome we have a dropping pH causing the separation of ligands and receptors. Receptors will be sent back to the plasma membrane by recycling and lipoproteins will be degraded into lysosomes. 
When this happens, colesterol moves from the periphery (where it was bound to receptors) and it can also be found in the center of the cell. The key is that receptors and ligands are sorted in different vesicles and they will be sent to different places. Clathrin will also take part in this process. 
2. Degradation: This would be the most typical fate, and it is what happens to most endocytic contents. This involves the formation of a late endosome and its eventual fusion with lysosomes, to destroy all the contents that have been internalized. For example ligands have to be assimilated and for that they are degraded. Cells can make use of amino acids, sugars, lipids, … 
3. Retrograde transport to GA/ER: The retrograde transport of the contents is also possible. It is important to remember that endosomal products can move from the PM to GA, and then to the ER, at any time. Some endosomes make use of retrograde transport to bring their products back to the golgi and even the ER. In that case, they will be transferred in retrograde transport vesicles, which will be coated with COPI (KDEL). 
4. Transcytosis: This means they perform endocytosis on one side and exocytosis on the other side. Shortly after internalization, contents can be directly transferred to another membrane domain. This is typical in epithelial cells which have different membrane domains (for example the intestinal epithelial cell) they have the absorptive area, where they uptake nutrients and they have the basolateral membrane domains where those nutrients are released. In the case of enterocytes, there would be a transfer from the apical domain to the basolateral domain. These cells will have a great amount of transcytotic vesicles since they need to move nutrients. 
Whatever the case, the sorting of products is made at the early endosome level. 
**2.2. Synaptic vesicles: 
A particular case of recycling endosomes has been found in synaptic vesicles. Synaptic vesicles are recycling endosomes which undergo continuous cycles of exocytosis and endocytosis (that’s why we say that they are recycling vesicles)
Neurotransmitters filled by specific transporters of the vesicular membrane. Then, these vesicles will be bound to the actin sound of the nerve terminal. The release of the content of these vesicles will activate snare proteins. 
Those neurotransmitters in order to be released, the neuron has to find an action potential. Most of those vesicles are very close to the membrane, so the vesicle membrane and the target membrane are almost touching together. The only remaining step is the activation of the neuroproteins, just to make the fusion of membranes and the release of the transport. This membrane fusion happens thanks to the calcium entry (Ca2+). 
The calcium rise always comes because of changing membrane voltage, in other words, the activation of voltage in calcium channels will cause a rise in the intracellular calcium level. This calcium rise causes the fusion of the vesicle membrane with the plasma membrane. 
Once the content of these vesicles is released, the vesicle gets immediately internalized. This involves the participation of clathrin to get the formation of the endosome, as this one is empty now, refilled again. For that, clathrin protein vesicles help so synaptic vesicles are refilled back with neurotransmitters. This way, they become functional and can undergo the cycle again. So, technically, synaptic vesicles are recycling vesicles. 
*When we see a nerve terminal, the synaptic part is full of those vesicles, so this is a good way to distinguish nerve terminals (looking at the vesicle inside). It is also true that in the synaptic bottom we can find some vesicles coming from the GA, however, most of the vesicles will be synaptic vesicles.
**3. EARLY AND LATE ENDOSOMES: 
Endolysosomes are very heterogeneous membrane compartments. 
**Early endosome: 
-1 min average life 
- Twisted morphology
-Peripheral location, close to the plasma membrane 
-pH: 6 due to the progressive entry of proton pumps
They are very dynamic as they are constantly fusing with other endosomes. As the endosomes mature, they progressively become late endosomes. These ones are filled with intraluminal vesicles (that is how we can recognise them) and they are also known as AKA multivesicular bodies (MVB). 
**Late endosomes/AKA Multivesiuclar bodies: 
-5-15 min average life. 
-more central location 
-pH: 5 due to the entry of more proton pumps
These are recognizable for the presence of intraluminal vesicles. Those intraluminal vesicles are fundamental to degrade transmembrane proteins. As the luminal side of the endosome gets glycosylated (which will protect the membrane from degradation of lysosomal enzymes), so enzymes only degrade the endosomal content. 
Otherwise, there would be no way to degrade them (because at the late endosomal membrane they get glycosylated it is resistant to enzymatic  degradation). 
They have an average of 5-15 minutes and they move towards the central part. Their pH is low, about 5-5,5. The final fate of late endosomes will be to fuse with lysosomes to form what are called endolysosomes. The endosomes that form near the plasma membrane are gradually moved toward a more central location in the cell.
**4. ENDOSOMAL MATURATION: 
As we have mentioned before, as endosomes mature, they start getting filled with intraluminal vesicles. That happens for a reason: to accomplish the degradation of transmembrane proteins. Otherwise, if it stayed in the endosomal membrane it would be impossible to degrade it. 
In the end of endosomal maturation, when the endosomes fuse with lysosomes to create endolysosomes, the lysosomal enzymes will degrade everything that is in the lumen of the endolysosome. Both the endosome membrane and the lysosome membrane have a very thick layer of glycocalyx on their inner surface (made up of glycosyltransferase enzymes) that protects them from being broken down by the hydrolytic enzymes (like the lipases that break down lipids).  
If a receptor or transmembrane protein stayed directly on the outer membrane of the endosome, the enzymes released by the lysosome when it fuses wouldn’t be able to reach it because of the inner glycocalyx barrier.  
That’s why proteins that need to be destroyed are first tagged (usually with ubiquitin) and then sequestered toward the inside through invaginations that form intraluminal vesicles.  
Once inside the endosomal lumen (inside these vesicles), the proteins are fully exposed and accessible to be effectively degraded.
In other words, when an endosome fuses with a lysosome, the internal contents are degraded, while the proteins that are in the PM no. That's because the layer of carbohydrates that prevents the degradation of the lysosomal wall, which will be continuous with the endosomal wall. To degrade transmembrane proteins of the endosome, they must be enclosed into intraluminal vesicles.
The appearance of intraluminal vesicles is related to the fact that transmembrane proteins need to be sent to the lumen of the endolysosome. 
**5. THE ENDOSOME/LYSOSOME SYSTEM 
In the image, we can see the progressive maturation from early endosomes to late endosomes. In early endosomes there is a lot of activity. Some endosomes will carry out recycling of some specific products like membrane receptors, other contents will be sent to late endosomes… There is a progressive drop in pH, and appearance of intraluminal vesicles. These late endosomes will fuse with lysosomes to carry out the degradation of those products (cellular digestion). 
*Here we also can see the progressive movement of the endosome from the periphery to the central region. In order to move from the periphery to the central part, those endosomes usually bind to cytoskeletal fibers (microtubules), which will bring them to the central location. 
In early endosomes, they may get the possibility to send contents to the intracellular space, where you may get recycling, retrograde transport… 
In the last place, it is important to remember that once the late endosome fuses with the lysosome, degradation of the endosomal contents will happen. In addition, the interexchange of those contents with the GA and the ER will be possible at any step.

**7- How can we morphologically distinguish between an early and a late endosome?
Early and late endosomes represent distinct functional stages along the endocytic pathway. Early endosomes are relatively small, highly dynamic organelles located near the cell periphery, close to the plasma membrane. They act as the primary sorting hubs of the cell, continuously fusing with incoming endocytic vesicles to process internalized surface receptors and extracellular cargo. As these organelles mature, they migrate toward the cell interior along the microtubule network. Late endosomes are larger, more stable structures situated deeper within the center of the cell, in the perinuclear region. A defining hallmark of late endosomes is the presence of numerous intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within their lumen, earning them the designation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs). These intraluminal vesicles are crucial for sorting and sequestering transmembrane proteins destined for degradation away from the cytosol prior to final fusion with lysosomes. 
**8- What happens to the pH of the endosomal compartment, as it matures?
As the endosomal compartment matures, its pH progressively drops (acidifies) due to the incorporation of more proton pumps:
Initial/Early stage: The pH starts at around 7 units.
Progressive drop: It drops down to 6 units.
Late endosome: It decreases further to 5 to 5.5 units.
Endolysosome: Finally, when they reach the endolysosome, the pH typically falls to less than 5 units.

**10- What is transcytosis? Can it be performed by any cell in the body?
Transcytosis is a phenomenon of endocytosis at one side (the lumen area) and fast exocytosis at the opposite side. This facilitates the transfer of nutrients from the capillary lumen to the tissues. This is a very typical process in endothelial cells. It is also common in enterocytes. This is involved in the transport of nutrients.

1. THE APPARATUS  
1.1. Discovery  
The Golgi apparatus: vital component of the endomembrane systems (important in vesicle 
trafficking, it decides where vesicles will go). 
 
ORIGIN 
●​ It is named after Camillo Golgi (19th century) (he shared the Nobel prize with Ramón y Cajal) 
who discovered it in 1898.  
●​ He developed the silver impregnation technique: it involves the precipitation of specific 
metals (silver metals) in the areas rich in the Golgi apparatus, allowing them to be stained 
and visualised.  He used stained Spinal Ganglion neurons  
 
In some Spinal ganglion neurons dark spots show silver precipitates (the Golgi Apparatus). 0

1.2. Structure  
●​ Stack of membranous compartments (cisternae “cada uno de los saquitos”), independent 
but pulled tight together. 
●​ Stack of quite flat systems (cisternae) which are close to each other (tightly piled), but 
not connected (they are independent).  
●​ The golgi apparatus can be also called “Golgi complex” 
 
Function: director of vesicle trafficking: 
●​ It classifies and packs proteins in different outgoing vesicles (according to their final 
destination) 
●​ Considered as the director of vesicle trafficking. 
 
PROCESS OF VESICLE TRAFFICKING (medio resumido) 
1.​ It receives contents from the ER in the form of vesicles 
2.​ Those vesicles fuse with the CIS side of the GA. 
3.​ Then, they are delivered into more specific vesicles of the TRANS side.  
4.​ By the time the products get out of the cell, they are classified according to their final 
destination.  
 
The components that are inside of the vesicles are highly altered by:  
●​ Sulfatations 
●​ Glycosylations  
●​ Modifications… 
 
This components will have different glycosylation patterns (added in the Golgi Apparatus) 
depending on the destination of the vesicle carrying them.   
 
By TEM we can see how the CIS side is convex and the TRANS concave.

THE GOLGI NETWORK  
The inside of these cisterns is called lumen.  
The golgi apparatus will receive vesicles which come from the ER (endoplasmic reticulum). 
●​ Cis-Golgi network (CGN): area formed by the vesicles that fuse with the CIS area. (These 
vesicles come from the ER) 
●​ Trans-Golgi network (TGN): Vesicles coming out of the TRANS area that will be delivered 
to their final destination. 
 
In the inside of the apparatus: those vesicles will have to cross those cisternae while some 
reactions take place, helping the sorting and classification of the products into different vesicles 
according to their final destination.  
●​ Proteins will be glycosylated during their passage (proteins with a similar glycosylation 
pattern will be stored in the same TGN vesicles) 
Each cistern has a different glycosyl transfer reaction. Basically the GA is a giant sugar 
transferring machine, because it adds sugar residues to different proteins.We can see which 
enzymes are in each of the compartments.  
 
1.3. Functions  
The GA participates in:  
●​ Lipid and glycolipid biosynthesis (particularly in sphingolipid synthesis) 
●​ Protein glycosylation and modification. (Glycosylations pose the end of the 
proteins’ maturation) 
●​ Lysosomal biogenesis (lysosomes emerge from the trans Golgi side)  
●​ Main function: Sorting of cargo proteins into different vesicles.  
Cargo proteins: proteins which are transported from one organelle to another through 
vesicles. 
THE DIRECTOR OF INTRACELLULAR VESICLE TRAFICKING 
The Golgi Apparatus is a polarized organelle because it has 2 sides: 
●​ CIS side (convex side): the receptor of the products that come in from the ER, fusing with 
the Cis area of the Golgi complex “Cis-Golgi network” (CGN) 
●​ TRANS side: the “expulsor”, which means that products leave the GA from that side. 
The products which enter the apparatus from the CIS side are not classified (the side where 
products are incorporated is called Cis-Golgi Network or CGN).

Vesicles which come out of the TRANS area (the part from which vesicles leave the GA is the 
Trans-Golgi Network or TGN) are classified into different vesicles.  
How? Thanks to the reactions which take place in the different compartments (cisternae) 
and to the glycosylation patterns which are added to the products during these reactions 
(these products will be embedded to the vesicular membrane).  
GA is called the director of intracellular vesicle trafficking: thanks to the reactions which 
take place there, vesicles will be classified depending on their final destination (depending 
on their glycosylation pattern, realmente los que tienen estos patrones son las moléculas 
que transportan). 
Their glycosylation pattern will determine which receptors (specific for each pattern) will 
bind. Consequently, different vesicles will form to transport them to their respective 
destination. 
Contents (ex. Cargo proteins) will be transferred progressively from one cistern to another 
and, the ones leaving, are ready to deliver sorted products. Vesicles will deliver the material 
by fusing their membranes.  
By the time vesicles and products go out of the TRANS side: they are sorted out in different 
vesicles due to their sugar pattern (glycosylations), which is distinguishable and identified by 
different cells.  
The vesicles leaving the TGN that will be transported to the same organelle: 
●​ The components they have inside have similar modification patterns (sugar patterns, for 
example) 
The vesicles leaving the TGN that have different destinations: 
●​ They have different sorted products (with different modification patterns) and they are 
segregated in different vesicles.

1.4. Movement of contents across the GA : transfer of membranes 
There are 2 mechanisms which coexist:  
 
1. CISTERNAL MATURATION MODEL  
●​ The GA constantly receives new membranes from the ER (this means that it will be 
continuously supplied with new membrane material). 
 
This constant input leads to the formation of new membrane compartments (cisternae) that 
fuse with the CIS face of the Golgi (CGN), which is therefore constantly being renewed.  
 
As new cisternae form at the cis face, older ones are gradually displaced/pushed toward the 
trans face. During this movement, each cisterna matures: 
●​ It changes its protein and enzyme composition as it progresses from the cis to the trans 
side.  
●​ There is a net movement of contents.  
This process has important implications (each cisternae contains a distinct set of enzymes 
responsible for specific modifications of proteins and lipids): 
●​ The cisternae themselves move forward: these resident enzymes must be recycled to 
maintain the functional organization of the Golgi.  
●​ This is achieved through retrograde transport, in which vesicles carry enzymes backward 
(from later to earlier cisternae) allowing each compartment to retain its characteristic 
enzymatic composition even as the membranes themselves mature and move forward.  
**Resident proteins, therefore, must be retrotransported to keep their place in their appropriate 
system. 
Explicación más simple: según van madurando los compartimentos del AG por el movimiento 
que se genera, las enzimas características de cada compartimento se mueven hacia atrás (sentido 
contrario a la maduración de las cisternae), para que se mantenga cada enzima en su cisternae 
correspondiente. 
Este movimiento es retrograde transport  
 
2. VESICLE TRANSPORT MODEL  
 
Vesicles form and bud off at the edges of the Golgi apparatus. These vesicles transport 
materials both forward (anterograde) and backward (retrograde) between Golgi 
compartments.  
 
 

=== PAGE 8 ===
 
 
They shuttle contents from the trans side back to the cis side, or vice versa. Without this 
lateral (se le llama lateral pero en verdad se refiere a lo de antes) transport, materials would 
eventually be lost as new membranes push through the Golgi stack (this movement 
corresponds to cisternae maturation model).  
Retrograde transport is important because enzymes need to return to earlier Golgi 
compartments to maintain proper function. This ensures the Golgi retains its specific 
enzymatic composition despite the continuous movement of membranes.  
BOTH MODELS COEXIST  
 
2. PROTEIN GLYCOSYDATION IN THE GA  
Glycosylation of proteins begins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) 
The Golgi apparatus (GA) is mainly responsible for extensively modifying these glycosylation 
patterns.  
 
When proteins enter the cis-Golgi network (CGN): they all carry the same initial 
oligosaccharide consisting of 14 sugar residues attached to asparagine (Asn) residues via 
N-linked glycosylation.  
By the time the proteins reach the cis face of the Golgi, only a central part of this 
oligosaccharide remains, made up of 5 residues (de los 14 que había procedentes del RER, 
solo se mantienen 5):  
●​ 2 N-acetylglucosamines  
●​ 3 mannoses.  
This trimmed oligosaccharide is the basis for further modifications within the Golgi, which 
ultimately determines the final destination of the protein.  
There are two main glycosylation patterns on the Asn residues (these glycosylations attach to 
the central part): 
 

=== PAGE 9 ===
 
 
- Complex glycosylation: Addition of various sugars to the central oligosaccharide (such as 
galactose, N-acetylglucosamine, sialic acid, among others).  
●​ These modifications direct proteins to different destinations, but most proteins 
modified this way follow the secretory pathway (ending up as membrane 
(constitutive) or secreted proteins (regulated)).  
●​ The glycosylation patterns are highly variable and protein-specific.  
 
- Mannose glycosylation: Only mannose residues are added to the central oligosaccharide. 
Crucially, some of these mannoses are phosphorylated (mannose-6-phosphate).  
●​ The mannose phosphorylation acts as a tag that directs the protein to lysosomes (Como 
ponía en lysosomes), marking them as future lysosomal proteins. (key signal for lysosomal 
targeting) 
●​ This mannose phosphorylation is a HIGHLY SPECIFIC for lysosomal enzymes.

3. CONTROLLING THE FINAL DESTINATION OF PROTEINS (most 
important function)  
Proteins which exit the TGN through vesicles have 3 different main destinations (esto ya se ha 
explicado antes, pero más resumido):  
● LYSOSOMES: vesicles containing hydrolytic enzymes which are rich in phosphorylated 
mannose-6-phosphate.  
There are mannose-6-phosphate specific receptors (in one side of the TGN) waiting for 
the vesicles carrying mannose-6-phosphate groups, which acts as a tag. These 
vesicles will then bud off to form new lysosomes. 
● SECRETION VESICLES:  
○ CONSTITUTIVE SECRETION VESICLES (they don’t rely in any signal):  
●​ The default pathway.  
●​ They are targeted directly to the plasma membrane  
●​ There is no control of it, so they need no signal.  
●​ They are constantly produced and getting out of the cell.  
 
There are parts of transmembrane proteins that are facing the inside membrane of the 
vesicle that will end up facing the outside of the plasma membrane.  
Why? 
When this vesicle fuses with plasma membrane the transmembrane proteins that 
were facing will turn and face out. Which is why sugars are only found in the 
extracellular part.    
○ REGULATED SECRETION VESICLES:  
●​ they also contain specifically sorted products 
●​ they will be stored in the cytoplasm waiting for the release-signal.  
●​ When this appears, this will trigger the fusion of these vesicles with the 
plasma membrane to release these contents all at once.

● RETROGRADE TRANSPORT VESICLES: they go back to the Cis side of the Golgi

●​ They transport proteins which are needed in the ER. For example, chaperons

LYSOSOMAL BIOGENESIS IN THE TGN  
In the Golgi Complex, this process consists of different phases (each of which is carried out in 
different sides of the GA) 
●​ CGN: addition of the signal for lysosomal sorting (addition and phosphorylation of 
mannose) 
●​ TGN: recognition of this signal (proteins which are rich in mannose-6-phosphate bind to 
their specific receptors which are concentrated on specific sites of the TGN) 
○​ The signal is recognised when binding to M6P receptors which will be 
concentrated at specific sites of the TGN.  
○​ Those receptors will be segregated to specific parts of the TGN. 
○​ Clathrin usually associates with receptors and also assists the process of creating 
vesicles and it makes membranes bend.  
 
Many hydrolytic enzymes (vesicles) will fuse with each other to create primary 
lysosomes: the lumen of lysosomes will be acidic.  
●​ The acidification of the lysosomal lumen (because of the proton pumps) causes 
the dissociation of the ligand and the receptors.  
○​ Ligands (hydrolytic enzymes) will be concentrated in the lysosomal lumen. 
○​ Receptors will be recycled back to the TGN to participate in the 
recruitment of more hydrolytic enzymes.  
So in conclusion, we can say that the creation of lysosomes all depends on the concentration 
of lysosomal enzymes, as well as mannose-6-phosphate tags and MP6 receptors. Vesicles 
budding assisted by clathrin.

4. BIDIRECTIONAL COMMUNICATION BETWEEN ER AND GOLGI  
Retrograde transport of products from GA to ER is possible.  As is the case of chaperones.  
Retrograde transport vesicles: vesicles that will go through the whole GA back to the ER.  
●​ Those vesicles will be transporting ER resident proteins.  
●​ Proteins that get to the ER as their final destination are not synthesized and stay 
in the ER for the whole time, they have to go to the GA, and then, they will have 
to be retrieved back to the ER. We have 2 pathways (one forward and one 
backwards): 
  
1.​ Forward pathway or anterograde transport (Anterograde Transport):  transport from the 
ER to the Golgi. Vesicles which take part in this transport are called COPII vesicles. They 
contain a specific coat (COP II) and go from the ER to the GA.  
 
¿Qué es el “specific coat”? They mediate the formation of transport vesicles in the donor 
compartment. (Está bien explicado en el tema 9) 
 
2.​ Retrieval pathway or retrograde transport (Retrograde transport): consists of transport 
from the GA back to the ER. The vesicles which take part are COP I vesicles. It is used for 
the ER resident proteins. 
**The retrieval of proteins is mediated by KDEL receptors in the TGN. Proteins that 
need to be retrieved to the ER have a specific signal which consists of a short 
strand of 4 amino acids (Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu). This way COPI vesicles will identify the 
signal and take the protein back to the ER. It is a pH depending binding.  
 
The role of COPI and COPII is to facilitate vesicle formation.  
Yellow ones are secretory proteins and pink ones are ER resident proteins. Both proteins get 
together in the COPII vesicles to the CIS Golgi part, but then in the GA, secretory vesicle will 
be recognised and sent out by constitutive or regulated secretion. And ER proteins will be 
recognised by specific receptors that are facing the lumen of the TGN. Those receptors are 
called KDEL receptors. ER resident proteins will have a tag (KDEL) which consists of some 
short strands of 4 Aa which are Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu (KDEL). This KDEL signal will be exclusively in 
proteins which make part of the ER. Then, COPI vesicles containing KDEL receptors will bring

5. LIPID BIOSYNTHESIS IN THE GOLGI COMPLEX  
Sphingolipids are assembled in the GA. 
Sphingolipids are created by: 
●​ A ceramide (1 fatty acid + sphingosine): 
○​ The ceramide is synthesized in the SER 
○​ Gets translocated to the GA thanks to specific transporters.  
○​ This ceramide does not travel in vesicles, it gets translocated specifically.  
 
●​ Different polar groups (like sugars or phosphocholine).  
○​ Once in the GA, this ceramide may undergo 2 types of modifications. 
○​ The first modification: add sugars to it and generate sphingoglycolipids 
(gangliosides and cerebrosides) 
○​ Second one is: add phosphocholine creating sphingomyelin, which together with 
the phosphatidylcholine, are the most abundant lipids in our membranes. 
 
Shingoglycolipids and shingomyelin are both sphingolipids.

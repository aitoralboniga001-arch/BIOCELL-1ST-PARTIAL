**1. DIFFERENT DNA PACKING LEVELS
DNA has different packaging levels and it adopts its structure due to modifications within histones (only in eukaryotes) and other proteins. For instance during the interphase we can find the following:
Heterochromatin: When interactions within DNA and histones strengthen, the chromatin DNA fiber (30 nm) is formed. The DNA is too condensed for RNA polymerase to enter and, therefore, transcription is impossible. 
Euchromatin: Nucleosome DNA fiber (10 nm). It is composed of individual nucleosomes. It’s more accessible for RNA polymerase and, therefore, transcription can occur.
DNA packaging is very dense in the nucleus, as human cells contain 2 meters of DNA; therefore, it needs to be very condensed to be enclosed in the tiny space of the nucleus (of about 5 µm3). This packaging is accomplished thanks to the binding with proteins (histones and other proteins).
Moreover, the maximum packaging in the form of chromosomes can be found during cell division. This is when you can distinguish chromosomes (picture).
Euchromatin is coiled around nucleosomes (formed by histones) and then compacted into heterochromatin. This fiber forms loops that may be associated with each other and will reach maximum condensation during cell division when it forms chromosomes. 
Histones are very complex proteins.
**2. CHROMATINE STRUCTURE
The DNA is coiled around nucleosomes, and each of them contains 8 histones (octamer) . There are different types of histones (H2A, H2B, H3, H4 ×2). They are fundamental to protecting DNA from mutations and they regulate how DNA is transferred. That’s why eukaryotes have a lower rate of mutation than procaryotes.
Histone H1 is located between nucleosomes. This structure regulates the level of packaging.
Histones are positively charged, highly conserved between species, and stabilize DNA. Thanks to their concentration of lysine and arginine (and NLS). Due to being positively charged, they can interact very tightly with DNA as DNA is negatively charged.

**5. EPIGENETIC REGULATION: HISTONE ACETYLATION
In order to be transcribed, genes need to be “unpacked” so they can be read by RNA polymerases: Loosen and open chromatin.
Modifications affecting DNA-histone compaction: 
Acetylation (distorts electrical charge): Histones are positively charged. By acetylations, the histones stop being positively charged. This will weaken interactions between histones and DNA, and favours opening.
Histone Acetyl Transferases (HAT), enzymes present in the nucleus, cause chromatin to unpack for gene activation. They transfer an acetyl group (–COCH₃) from acetyl-CoA to lysine residues on histone tails.
Histone Deacetylases (HDAC) chromatin condensation to avoid transcription. They remove acetyl groups and they restore the positive charge of the histones so they can interact strongly with the DNA.
**Gene repression (HDACs)
Modifications are reversible and don't involve any change in the DNA. 
**6. POST-TRANSLATIONAL MODIFICATIONS IN HISTONES
A promotes gene expressions
Methylations can be introduced in different residues. They can be activating or inhibitory, whereas acetylations are always activating. Histone methylation involves the addition of methyl groups to lysine or arginine residues by histone methyltransferases (HMTs). Unlike acetylation, methylation does not alter charge but instead modifies the binding affinity of chromatin-associated proteins. Methyl marks are reversible, removed by histone demethylases (HDMs).
Phosphorylation can also be either activating or inhibitory. Histone phosphorylation occurs when kinases attach phosphate groups to some residues on histones. The addition of these negatively charged groups alters chromatin structure and serves as a signal in key cellular processes. This modification is reversible, as phosphatases can remove the phosphate groups, restoring the previous state.
Kinases: is a type of enzyme that transfers a phosphate group from a high-energy molecule (usually ATP) to a specific substrate

**12. CHROMATIN LOOPS, TADs, AND COMPARTMENTS
There are key components that help the genome to acquire its three-dimensional organization (3D): TADs, Cohesin and CTCF factors.
Chromatin can adopt different states, ranging from heterochromatin to euchromatin. During this process, chromatin loops are formed, which define Topologically Associating Domains (TADs). TADs bring specific genomic regions into close physical proximity, allowing local interactions.
Loops are generated by the coordinated action of Cohesin and CTCF. Cohesin forms the space vector ring that allows DNA loops to form. CTCF proteins, meanwhile, mark the boundaries of those loops.
By isolating DNA into distinct domains, chromatin can be segregated into different compartments. Regions that are transcriptionally inactive (not expressed) are typically associated with the B compartment, while active (expressed) regions are enriched in the A compartment.
These loops can physically separate chromosomes. Even when they remain within their own territory of the chromosome,  different parts of those chromosomes need to be spread and they move physically to the centre of the loops. So cohesin encircles chromatin fibres making loops.
This may allow bringing distant parts of the genome of different chromosomes close together by the centre  of the loops. Then, CTCF factors are what define the TAD factors. So by encompassing the CTCF protein, the cohesin won't pass any more DNA across.
**13. S/MAR BANDINGND ANCHORES CHROMATIN LOOP
This organization can lead to synergistic gene activation. Topologically Associating Domains (TADs) can interact with each other by anchoring to the nuclear scaffold or matrix.
Scaffold/Matrix Attachment Regions (S/MARs) anchor these domains to the physical architecture of the nucleus.
By binding to these structural elements, chromatin loops may form cooperative associations. This enables regions from different chromosomes to be brought into close proximity at the nuclear matrix. As a result, transcriptional co-activators and regulatory elements can be concentrated in the same nuclear neighborhood.
In this way, TADs can be reorganized or clustered to promote or repress the expression of specific gene groups. Genes that need to be expressed as part of a physiological response may be brought together through such loop-matrix interactions. Very often, the binding to the neutral selectors determines the synergistic associations in these domains.
Very often, binding to the nuclear scaffold determines these synergistic associations, leading to the coordinated activation of gene networks that may span one or multiple chromosomes.

**14. THE NUCLEOLUS
The nucleolus is an electrondense structure that can be observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Its main function is the assembly of ribosomes. This involves the transcription of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes from the chromosomes and the subsequent processing and association of these rRNAs with ribosomal proteins to form the subunits of mature ribosomes.
A nucleus may contain one or several nucleoli. This occurs because rRNA gene clusters (nucleolar organizer regions, NORs) are located on different chromosomes. When these chromosomal regions are transcriptionally active and come together, multiple nucleoli can form. In general, the number and size of nucleoli correlate with the level of ribosome biogenesis, and thus with the protein synthesis activity of the cell. 
If a cell needs to produce a lot of proteins, it is more likely to have more than one nucleolus.
**15. NUCLEOLAR FUNCTION: Ribosome biosynthesis
Within the nucleolus, three distinct regions can be identified:
Fibrillar Center (FC): These appear as lighter (less electron-dense) areas under TEM. They contain euchromatin regions with ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. They look pale because the DNA here is transcriptionally active and less condensed.
Dense Fibrillar Component (DFC): Surrounding the fibrillar centers, this region appears darker (more electron-dense). It contains newly transcribed pre-rRNA molecules and processing factors involved in the early steps of rRNA maturation.
Granular Component (GC): This region is less dense than the DFC and occupies the rest of the nucleolus. It is where later stages of ribosome assembly take place, as rRNA combines with ribosomal proteins to form pre-ribosomal subunits.
Altogether, the nucleolus represents an accumulation of chromosomal regions carrying rRNA genes (nucleolar organizer regions, NORs), along with the machinery required for ribosome biogenesis.
**16. RIBOSOMAL PROTEINS MAKE A "Return trip” FROM CYTOPLASM TO NUCLEOLUS
Within the nucleolus, chromosomal regions carrying ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes cluster together. These genes are transcriptionally active because they are located in euchromatic regions. As rRNA is produced, it undergoes processing and eventually associates with ribosomal proteins to form ribosomal subunits.
Ribosomal proteins are synthesized in the cytosol and must travel back into the nucleus. Each ribosomal protein contains a nuclear localization signal (NLS), which allows its import through the nuclear pore complexes with the help of importins.
Once inside the nucleolus, ribosomal proteins are assembled with the newly transcribed rRNAs to form the small and large ribosomal subunits. These pre-ribosomal subunits are then exported to the cytoplasm via the nuclear pores. Export requires nuclear export signals (NES) and specific export factors.
